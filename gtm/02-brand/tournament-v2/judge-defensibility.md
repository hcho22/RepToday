# Judge: Defensibility and Legal/Claims Risk

Blind judge, tournament v2.
Read: the four pitches in `tournament-v2/`, plus `pain-point-frequency.md`, `meta-ad-library-sweep.md`, `name-collisions.md`, `product-facts-brief.md`, `platform-signal-evidence.md`, `competitors-additional.md`, and the Freeletics, Sweat, Ladder, and Down Dog teardowns to verify cited claims.
Reference reader: a 9pm scroller with zero context, and behind them a hostile paraphraser, a competitor's counsel, and an App Store reviewer.

## 1. Published rubric (written before scoring)

| # | Criterion | Weight | Definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | Claims hygiene and substantiation posture | 30 | Every factual and competitor claim is supportable by the cited research, bounded to what was observed, and survives FTC substantiation scrutiny. No invented proof, no unqualified universals, no health/medical claims the product-facts brief prohibits, no data-practice misstatements. Comparative claims about named competitors must match the teardown files verbatim. |
| C2 | Durability over 24 months | 25 | Will the lead still be true, and still be ownable, in 24 months? Penalizes leads that depend on competitor inaction, on a contested word, or on territory already being claimed by others. Rewards leads whose defense is a product mechanic rather than a sentence anyone can utter. |
| C3 | Brand-safety of register under hostile paraphrase | 20 | Does any line drift toward shame, grind, or bro-fitness when truncated, screenshotted, or paraphrased by a hostile reader? A lead word that is only safe with a chaperone sentence loses points here, because 6-second formats delete chaperones. |
| C4 | Trademark and name posture | 15 | The pitch's name verdict must reason soundly from `name-collisions.md`, must not imply clearance anywhere, and gets credit for recognizing descriptive-name risk versus coined-name strength. Any implied clearance is a hard flag. |
| C5 | Guardrail compliance and internal honesty | 10 | Honors the research guardrails (Nike Training Club free-tier caveat, Duolingo-quote mechanism-not-quote rule), the voice rules, the zero-users honesty requirement, and tags assumptions as assumptions. |

Weights sum to 100.

## 3. Per-pitch judgments

### Pitch 1: Discipline inversion (rank 3, 7.35)

Strongest: the most rigorous self-audit in the tournament - every risk I would have raised (semantic capture, overpromise, thin decision-fatigue evidence, copyability) is already stated plainly in its own section 7, and every competitor characterization checks out against the teardowns.
Weakest, and it is structural: the lead word is only safe with its inversion attached, and this pitch concedes in its own risk list that truncation miscodes the brand toward the drill-sergeant pole that owns the word in-market (the Jillian Michaels ad is the only observed discipline creative).
Under my lens that is a permanent compliance tax on every surface, forever, which caps C3 at 6 no matter how clean the flagship hook is.
Claims flag (soft): "carries the discipline for you" sits one hostile paraphrase from "the app will make you work out," a behavioral-outcome claim no app can substantiate; the pitch names this exact risk and supplies the mitigation rule, which is why C1 stays at 8 rather than dropping.
No truth-policy or guardrail violations found; the name section is honest and states clearance UNVERIFIED.

### Pitch 2: Anti-discipline / already-ready (rank 1, 7.95)

Strongest: it is the only pitch whose every hook line is proven by the footage within two seconds, which is the best possible substantiation posture - the claim and the evidence are the same asset, and a pre-launch brand with zero proof stack can defend it indefinitely.
Its register is the safest in the set: no contested word, no enemy named at the surface, nothing for a hostile reader to paraphrase into shame.
Hard flag on the primary hook: "Every workout app asks how long you have" is an unqualified universal that the pitch's own cited research falsifies - Ladder serves coach-fixed sessions without asking, and Wakeout asks outcome and location, not duration; this must ship as "Most workout apps" or use the clean variant openers the pitch already provides.
Soft flags: "nothing to buy" in the positioning statement needs care against the ~$7.99/mo premium and App Store IAP disclosure, and the pillar-5 line "trains the stiffness from your desk" edges toward the relief claims the product-facts brief prohibits.
Weakest on durability: the pitch admits it has no moat sentence and that the claim is cheap for a competitor to say; its honest answer (the demo and their configure-first reality) is real but earns only a 6 on C2.

### Pitch 3: Showing up (rank 2, 7.65)

Strongest: the best durability argument in the tournament - it correctly reasons that absence claims ("no streaks") are copyable in an afternoon while affirmative mechanics (rolling score, celebrated Return, Re-entry Ramp, 5-minute full show-up) are not, and it is the only pitch that names the indie-builder contest on anti-streak language and answers it.
Hard flag: the pillar-1 sample line "That is the only fact the app records" is false as written - the app records session history, a Consistency Score, analytics, and syncs via CloudKit - and a false data-practices statement is exactly the category of line that draws FTC and App Store privacy scrutiny; it must be cut or rewritten as a scoring claim, not a recording claim.
Soft flags: the first-person hook ("I skipped four days and my workout app congratulated me") requires the speaker to be identified as the founder under endorsement-disclosure rules, and "no trial that flips to a charge on the workouts" is slippery under truncation because the premium 14-day trial presumably does convert.
The reclamation of "all you have to do is show up" is clever but sits one edit away from the Jillian Michaels register it borrows from, which is why C3 is 8, not 9.
Guardrail compliance is exemplary: the Duolingo mechanism-not-quote rule is honored explicitly in the hook rationale.

### Pitch 4: Contrarian refusal (rank 4, 6.40)

Strongest: the most heavily cited pitch in the set, with every competitor mechanic it attacks verified in the teardowns, and an honest "head start, not a wall" assessment of its own moat.
Weakest under this lens on three independent axes.
First, claims: "that machinery serves the app's retention curve, not the user's consistency" is an intent claim about named competitors that no fetched research can substantiate - mechanics are provable, motives are not - and building creative on named enemies plus their negative App Store reviews invites Lanham Act comparative-advertising scrutiny the moment it leaves the strategy doc.
Second, durability: the position structurally depends on Freeletics, Sweat, and Ladder keeping their machinery, and the pitch itself concedes the anti-streak language is being claimed by indie builders while we wait.
Third, register: an oppositional lead spends the calm-brand equity the voice rules exist to protect, and the pitch's own gag-collapse risk is the hostile-paraphrase failure mode C3 measures; one screenshot of a refusal line without its mechanic reads as an angry anti-fitness account.
No fabricated evidence and the guardrails are honored, so this is a well-built pitch for a thesis my rubric prices as the riskiest.

## 4. The discipline question

**Verdict: internal spine only. Not the surface lead word. Not dropped.**

Judged on merit against this rubric, the case is one-sided.
The only observed in-market use of discipline language is the drill-sergeant register ("your no-excuses solution"), so a zero-equity brand leading with the word inherits a hostile prior it must argue out of in six seconds.
Pitch 1's own risk list and Pitch 2's compression argument converge on the same fact: the inversion is safe only with its chaperone sentence, and the formats that matter delete chaperones, which makes the surface word a permanent brand-safety liability under hostile paraphrase - a C3 problem no execution discipline fully retires.
The pain corpus never uses the word: users say decisions, resets, and paywalls, and matching their vocabulary is the cheapest substantiation a proof-less brand can buy.
Durability also argues against the surface: a contested word can be recaptured by the register that already owns it, while the mechanics (ready-on-open, rolling score, five minutes counts) stay true and demonstrable for as long as the product ships them.
But dropping discipline entirely would be wrong: the product-facts brief defines the product by it ("discipline means showing up, and showing up is made easy"), it correctly names the design philosophy that generated every mechanic, and it belongs in the manifesto, founder interviews, and week-2 retention surfaces where there is room for the full inversion.
The word is the architecture; the surface should ship the building.

## 5. Name verdict

**RepToday stands; no challenger beats it.**
Under a defensibility rubric the reasoning is mostly about what the alternatives cost: Cairn carries a live same-category App Store collision ("Cairn - Hiking Safety Tracker", Health & Fitness) which is exactly the confusion posture that invites both user loss and opposition risk, and Stack is saturated past recovery (exact-name game at 56K+ ratings, Stack Team App, Stack Sports), while RepToday's scan is the cleanest on record - no close variant in US App Store search, NXDOMAIN on reptoday.app, and the main web collision (REP Fitness) is an equipment retailer in a different trade channel.
The honest caveat my lens adds and no pitch fully prices: "Rep Today" is built from two common descriptive words, so it will be a suggestive-at-best mark - harder to register and enforce than a coined name - and the crowded "Rep"-prefix tracker space means the defensive perimeter is narrow; the mitigation is that the brand's moat was never the mark, it is the mechanics, and a coined name would trade real findability and meaning for enforcement strength this business does not need at pre-launch.
All four pitches correctly state that trademark and App Store clearance remain UNVERIFIED and none implies clearance, so no pitch takes the C4 hard flag; the USPTO search and App Store Connect reservation stay the founder's mandatory next action before any commitment.
One unanimous and correct finding I endorse with a defensibility rationale on top: kill the listing suffix "Rest Tomorrow" - beyond fighting every winning lead, it hands a hostile reader a grind-culture paraphrase ("no rest until tomorrow") that undoes the brand-safety work the rest of the copy pays for.
